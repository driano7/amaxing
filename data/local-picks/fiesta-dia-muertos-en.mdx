# Day of the Dead: when the city lights up its memory

From October 31 to November 2, Mexico City changes the subject. This is not
Halloween: here death does not disguise itself, it is visited. You go to the
cemetery, you light a candle, you eat pan de muerto and you remember
someone by name.

It is one of the country's most important festivals, and in 2008 it was
recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.

## What to do: ten things, north to south

1. **Mixcoac Cemetery.** The largest in Latin America. The custom is simple
   and the spectacle is the light: thousands of candles burning on the
   graves on November 1 and 2. Go at night, and go carefully: respect for
   the dead here is real.
2. **The Hall of Fame inside Mixcoac.** The area of the famous graves,
   where you find the people who built this country. It is the visit that
   makes the cemetery a place of memory and not only a cemetery.
3. **San Fernando Cemetery.** In Tacubaya. The most famous one in the city:
   Villavicencio, José María Morelos, Ignacio Manuel Altamirano. The marble and
   the trees do the rest.
4. **The altars across Mexico City.** Casa de los Azulejos, the Museo de
   Arte Popular, the Claustro de Sor Juana. Each one mounts its own altar
   and they are all different.
5. **Coyoacán.** Smaller and more intimate: the Alhóndiga de Toluca and the
   San Andrés cemetery. The green of the neighbourhood makes it a different
   kind of celebration.
6. **The National Commission altar.** The most honest thing you can see in
   Mexico City: the disappearance of tens of thousands of people in Mexico.
   The Commission's altar in the Centro is not decorative: it is a
   denunciation.
7. **The sugar skulls of La Merced.** Made by hand, one for each name. The
   old Mercado de la Merced is where you buy them and where you watch them
   being made.
8. **The Catrina parade.** A whole city dressed as the elegant dead,
   dancing and posing in the Centro. La Catrina was born as "La Calavera
   Garbancera" by José Guadalupe Posada in 1910, and became the symbol
   Mexico decided to laugh with death.
9. **The masses for the dead.** In the churches of the Centro, the mass for
   the dead on the night of the 31st. The church is full and the candles are
   everywhere.
10. **The stands.** The last one, and the easiest to miss: the stands of
    **pan de muerto** and the hot drinks that go with it. Nobody says
    goodbye to a dead person with an empty stomach.

## What to eat

- **Pan de muerto.** The bread of the dead: soft, sweet, with sugar on top.
  It is eaten in family, and nobody eats it alone.
- **Tamales of the dead and atole.** Hot, sweet, with cinnamon. The tamal of
  the dead is smaller and goes with the drink.
- **Pozole.** The same one as September 16, because the Mexican calendar
  repeats: here the pozole is eaten on November 1 and 2.
- **Pumpkin in a mole or a stew,** and sweet **mushrooms** in cream.
- **Pumpkin chocolate** and **pan de muerto** with a cup of coffee or atole.

## Understanding Mexicans

Mexico does not celebrate death: it **visits** it. That is the difference
with Halloween, which is a joke about a stranger's death. Here it is
someone's grandfather, and you bring them what they liked.

Three things explain almost everything:

- **The altar is an altar, not a decoration.** It has water, salt, bread,
  fruit, candles and the photo of the person. It is addressed, and it is
  left there overnight.
- **The dead are still part of the family.** They are not in the past: they
  are in the memory of the house, and the meal on November 1 includes them.
- **The death that is laughed at is ours, and the death that is mourned is
  ours too.** The Catrina is elegant and satirical because Posada made her
  to mock the European upper classes, and Mexico turned her into the
  affectionate skeleton of the whole country.

And there is a grief that is not private. The National Commission's altar
remembers the tens of thousands who disappeared. On those dates, Mexico does
not only remember its dead, it complains about them.
