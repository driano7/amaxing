# September 16: the day the city gets on its feet

September 16 is not just another public holiday. It is the day Mexico City
stands up: the Zócalo fills with people from the early morning, the city
wakes up with the street as its decoration, and for a few hours the whole
country seems to be standing in the same square.

And yes: there is a Grito, a chorus, a flag and a lot of people hugging.

## Where

- **The balcony of the National Palace.** From there the Grito is led, in
  Plaza de la Constitución. You get let in at a particular entrance, and the
  square fills long before eleven.
- **The Zócalo floor.** The square becomes the living room of the night of
  the 15th: flags, families and children asleep on a tarp.
- **Paseo de la Reforma.** The military parade on the morning of the 16th,
  with the standards and the units.
- **Campo Marte.** The military parade starts and ends there: you see it up
  close, with the schoolchildren there too.
- **The Ángel de la Independencia.** In the middle of Reforma, with the
  usual crowd around it.
- **Moneda and Allende.** Where the 15th becomes a neighbourhood party and
  not only protocol.

## What to do

- **The Grito, on the night of the 15th.** In Plaza de la Constitución, the
  president of the republic addresses the crowd from the balcony of the
  National Palace. The band plays the national anthem and the crowd shouts
  "¡Viva México!" along with whoever is presiding. Arrive early: the square
  fills long before eleven.
- **The parade on the morning of the 16th.** Along Reforma and the avenue
  of the Constitution. You see the standards of the military units and
  children in their first-grade uniform.
- **The school recitals.** Many are elementary schoolchildren dressed in
  their best, and they are half the party.
- **The pavilions and the stalls.** Eat and drink wherever the street
  invents a place for it.
- **The Zócalo by day.** The square changes: the flag, the families, the
  children who have fallen asleep on the tarp.
- **The night of the 15th, anywhere else.** The party does not end at the
  Zócalo: it moves to the terraces and the parks.

## What to eat

- **Pozole.** With its lettuce, its onion, its lime. The dish of the date,
  and it is eaten early, before the parade.
- **Tlacoyos.** Blue corn, filled with beans, the real thing from the
  south of the city.
- **Tamales.** Or the sweet ones, which in the south of the city are a
  completely different thing.
- **Esquites and elotes.** Grilled on the street, with lime, cheese and
  chile.
- **Aguas frescas and horchata.** To wash down the pozole. And a **concha**
  or a **maracaná** for the sweet.
- **Atole de cajeta** for whoever gets up at 4 a.m. to watch the flag go
  up.

## Understanding Mexicans

The Grito is not a speech: it is an **act**. It has been performed in the
same spot, in the same way, since the nineteenth century, and the crowd knows
the moment it comes. That is why the whole square shouts on the same
second: it is not a spectacle you watch, it is one you step into.

And the 16th is also a family day. Whoever stayed in the city goes back to
their town or their grandparents' house, and the meal is the same one that
has been made for decades. The parade is the public part; the meal is the
private one. Both are the same holiday.

## Why it matters at Amaxing

Because the best stories in a city are the ones where something actually
happened. The 16th is one of them, and it is also the date on which the
country remembers that independence was a **collective** act. It stays
**pinned** at Amaxing for the same reason: this is not a recommendation, it
is a calendar.
