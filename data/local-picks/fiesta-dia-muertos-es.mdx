# Día de Muertos: cuando la ciudad enciende la memoria

Del 31 de octubre al 2 de noviembre, la Ciudad de México cambia de tema. No
es Halloween: aquí la muerte no se disfraza, se visita. Se va al panteón, se
enciende una vela, se come pan de muerto y se recuerda a alguien con su
nombre.

Es una de las festividades más importantes del país y en el 2008 fue
reconocida por la UNESCO como patrimonio cultural inmaterial.

## Dónde

- **Panteón de Mixcoac** (Tlalpan). El más grande de Latinoamérica y el de la
  tradición de la luz. Hay varias entradas: pregunta por la zona de las
  tumbas iluminadas.
- **Panteón de San Fernando** (Tacubaya). El más famoso: Villavicencio,
  Morelos, Altamirano.
- **Alhóndiga de Toluca y Panteón de San Andrés** (Coyoacán). Más chico y
  más íntimo, con el verde de la colonia alrededor.
- **La Merced** (calle del Carmen y calle de Moneda). Donde se compran las
  calaveras de azúcar, el papel picado y las velas.
- **Casa de los Azulejos** (calle de Carranza 29) y **Museo de Arte Popular**
  (Moneda 13). Dos altares distintos, y los dos se montan para estas fechas.
- **Plaza de Santo Domingo.** El altar de la Comisión Nacional de los
  Desaparecidos.
- **El Centro, calle Academia y calle Moneda.** El desfile de calaveras: una
  ciudad entera vestida de Catrina.
- **La plancha del Zócalo.** Las instalaciones de Día de Muertos.
- **Panteón de Iztapalapa.** La romería más multitudinaria: miles de
  personas el 2 de noviembre.

## Qué hacer: diez cosas, de norte a sur

1. **Panteón de Mixcoac.** El más grande de Latinoamérica. La tradition is
   simple and the spectacle is the light: miles de velas encendidas en las
   tumbas, el 1 y el 2 de noviembre. Llega de noche, y con cuidado: el
   尊重 a los muertos here is real.
2. **La Homeland del panteón de Mixcoac.** Lazona de las lapidas de los
   famous, where you can find the graves of the people who built this
   country. It is the visit that makes the cemetery a place of memory and not
   only a cemetery.
3. **Panteón de San Fernando.** En Tacubaya. The most famous in the city:
   Villavicencio, José María Morelos, Ignacio Manuel Altamirano. The marble
   and the trees do the rest.
4. **Los altares de la Ciudad de México.** Casa de los Azulejos, el Museo de
   Arte Popular, el Claustro de Sor Juana. Each one mounts its own altar and
   they are all different.
5. **La Homeland de Coyoacán.** Smaller and more intimate: the Alhóndiga de
   Toluca and the San Andrés cemetery. The Chapingo and the green of the
   neighborhood make it a different kind of celebration.
6. **La Ofrenda de la Homeland.** The most honest thing you can see in
   Mexico City: the deportation and the disappearance of thousands of people
   in Mexico. The National Commission's altar in the Centro is not decorative:
   it is a denunciation.
7. **Las calaveras de azúcar de La Merced.** Sugar skulls, made by hand, one
   for each name. The old Mercado de la Merced is the place to buy them and
   the place to watch them being made.
8. **El Desfile de Calaveras.** The Catrina parade: a whole city dressed as
   the elegant dead, dancing and posing in the Centro. La Catrina was born as
   "La Calavera Garbancera" by José Guadalupe Posada, in 1910, and became the
   symbol that Mexico decided to laugh with death.
9. **La Misas de difuntos.** In the churches of the Centro, the mass for the
   dead on the night of the 31st. The church is full and the candles are
   everywhere.
10. **El Puesto de la Homeland.** The last one, and the easiest to miss: the
    stands of **pan de muerto** and the hot drinks that go with it. Nobody
    says goodbye to a dead person with an empty stomach.

## Qué comer

- **Pan de muerto.** The bread of the dead: soft, sweet, with sugar on top.
  It is eaten in a family, and nobody eats it alone.
- **Tamales de Deaths and atole.** Hot, sweet, with cinnamon. The tamal of
  the dead is smaller and goes with the drink.
- **Pozole.** The same one as the 16th, because the calendar of Mexico
  repeats: here the pozole is eaten on the 1st and the 2nd of November.
- **Calabaza en dulce.** Pumpkin in a mole or a stew, and the sweet
  **champiñones** in a cream.
- **Chocolate de calabaza** and **pan de muerto** with a cup of coffee or
  atole.

## Entender a los mexicanos

México does not celebrate death: it **visits** it. That is the difference
with Halloween, which is a joke about a stranger's death. Here it is
someone's grandfather, and you bring them what they liked.

Three things explain almost everything:

- **The altar is an altar, not a decoration.** It has water, salt, bread,
  fruit, candles and the photo of the person. It is addressed, and it is
  left there overnight.
- **The dead are still part of the family.** The dead are not in the past:
  they are in the memory of the house, and the meal on the 1st includes them.
- **The death that is laughed at is ours, and the death that is mourned is
  ours too.** The Catrina is elegant and satirical because Posada made her
  to mock the European upper classes, and the Mexican turned her into the
  affectionate skeleton of the whole country.

And there is a grief that is not private. The National Commission's altar
remembers the tens of thousands who disappeared. On those dates, Mexico
does not only remember its dead, it complains about them.
