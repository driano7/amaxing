# New Year in Mexico City

New Year's Eve is not exclusive to Mexico, but the madness here really is on
another scale. Elsewhere the year ends with a glass; in Mexico City it ends
with the whole street lit up, food at midnight and people deciding the plan
hasn't even started yet.

## Where

- **The Zócalo floor.** The most obvious meeting point, and the most
  crowded.
- **Copacabana and the Reforma corridor.** Where the night spreads over
  several streets and you can hop from one concert to the next. In 2025 more
  than two hundred thousand people celebrated on the Paseo.
- **Glorieta de Insurgentes** (and the one on Palma). Where two years meet:
  the one coming in and the one going out, on the same corner.
- **La Merced and República de Uruguay.** The streets to the west, where the
  party turns down a notch and becomes a neighbourhood thing.
- **The ice rink on the Zócalo.** Set up and taken down every year: the
  place to skate in the middle of the street.

## What to do

1. **The Zócalo**, early, before it fills up.
2. **Reforma**, where the crowd concentrates.
3. **The Glorieta**, to watch the year leave and arrive on the same corner.
4. **The fireworks**: the programme and the launch point change every year,
   so check where they are.
5. **The twelve grapes.** One for each month of the year ahead, with or
   without cream.
6. **The hugs.** At midnight everybody gets a hug, and it is serious: you
   have to do it in the right spot, looking up, and without talking.
7. **The small hours**, between three and six, when the city relaxes and the
   taco stands open.

## What to eat

- **Eggs with salt**, at twelve sharp, with a promise for a better year.
- **Grapes and apple.**
- **Tamal sandwiches** at two in the morning, which is when the city is
  genuinely hungry.
- **Atole, chocolate and ponche**, for the December cold in the capital.
- **The family dinner**: chicken or turkey. The most expensive and most
  argued-over meal of the year, and every family has its own version.

## Understanding Mexicans

Mexican New Year is not just a calendar: it is a **joke**. We laugh at what
is coming, we mock the year that is ending, we make noise and we leave the
old year on an altar of paper and glitter. The joke is not festive: it is a
way of saying next year will be better, even if we do not fully believe it.

And there is something more important: New Year is spent **with family**,
even for most of us who are not with our family. You go home, you eat, you
toast and you call whoever is far away. The party may be elsewhere, but the
table is set anyway.
